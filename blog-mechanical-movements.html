How a Mechanical Movement Actually Works

A mechanical watch runs on stored energy and a carefully balanced set of moving parts, with no battery involved. It looks like magic on the wrist, but the underlying mechanism is understandable once it's broken into its core pieces.

The mainspring: where the energy lives

At the heart of a mechanical movement is the mainspring, a tightly coiled ribbon of metal that stores energy the way a wound-up toy does. Winding the crown, either by hand or through an automatic rotor, tightens this spring. As it slowly unwinds, it releases energy at a controlled rate through the rest of the movement, which is what actually powers the watch for hours or days.

The gear train: passing the energy along

The mainspring's energy doesn't go straight to the hands. It passes through a gear train, a series of small toothed wheels that step the energy down to a manageable rate and carry it toward the escapement. This is also where the different gear ratios that drive the hour, minute, and second hands are set up, each turning at its own correct speed relative to the others.

The escapement: the heartbeat of the watch

The escapement is the part responsible for the audible "tick" of a mechanical watch. It releases the gear train's energy in small, controlled bursts rather than all at once, which is what allows the watch to measure time in discrete, even steps instead of the hands simply spinning freely. The balance wheel, paired with a hairspring, oscillates back and forth at a fixed rate, and the escapement locks and unlocks in time with that oscillation.

The balance wheel: the actual timekeeper

If the mainspring is the fuel and the escapement is the release valve, the balance wheel is the actual clock. It swings back and forth at a regulated frequency, typically several beats per second, and this steady oscillation is what the rest of the movement is built around. A well-regulated balance wheel is the difference between a watch that keeps accurate time and one that drifts noticeably over a day.

Automatic versus manual winding

A manual movement needs to be wound by hand periodically, using the crown, to keep the mainspring tensioned. An automatic movement adds a rotor, a weighted half-circle that spins with the natural motion of your wrist, which winds the mainspring continuously through the day as you move. Automatic watches will still wind eventually and stop if left unworn for long enough, which is why watch winders exist for people who rotate between several automatic watches.

Why mechanical movements vary in accuracy

Unlike a quartz movement, which uses a battery-powered oscillating crystal for extremely consistent timekeeping, a mechanical movement's accuracy depends on manufacturing precision, regulation, and even factors like temperature and position. A well-regulated mechanical watch might gain or lose a handful of seconds a day, which is considered normal and even excellent for the type, rather than a flaw.

Why people still choose mechanical over quartz

Quartz movements are objectively more accurate for the cost. Mechanical movements are chosen instead for the craftsmanship involved, the visible motion of the parts through a display case back, and the fact that a well-maintained mechanical watch can run for generations with occasional servicing, rather than being replaced when a battery-dependent circuit eventually fails.

The takeaway

A mechanical watch is a small, self-contained energy system: a wound spring, a gear train that carries that energy forward, and an escapement and balance wheel that release it in precisely timed increments. Understanding that sequence makes the whole object feel less like magic and more like a genuinely elegant piece of engineering worth taking care of.
