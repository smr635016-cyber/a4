How to Choose Your First Quality Watch

Buying a first "real" watch can feel more complicated than it needs to be, mostly because there's a lot of marketing language layered over what are actually fairly simple decisions. Here's what's worth focusing on.

Decide on movement type first

Quartz movements are accurate, low-maintenance, and generally more affordable. Mechanical movements — manual or automatic — cost more, require occasional winding or regular wear, and appeal more to people who value the craft and motion involved. Neither is objectively better; they suit different priorities, so decide which matters more to you before comparing specific watches.

Case size should match your wrist, not a trend

Case diameter trends shift over time, but a watch that's too large or too small for your wrist looks and feels wrong regardless of what's currently fashionable. As a rough guide, most adult wrists suit something between 36mm and 42mm, though this varies with wrist size and personal preference. Trying watches on in person, rather than judging purely from photos, is the most reliable way to gauge this.

Water resistance ratings matter more than they seem

A rating of 30 meters offers only basic splash resistance, not swimming protection, despite what the number might suggest casually. 50 to 100 meters is generally suitable for swimming, and 200 meters or more is aimed at serious diving or water sports. Match the rating to how you'll actually use the watch rather than assuming any water resistance rating covers any activity.

Sapphire crystal versus mineral glass

The crystal covering the dial affects how well a watch resists scratches over years of wear. Sapphire crystal is significantly more scratch-resistant than mineral glass, though it can be slightly more prone to shattering under a hard direct impact. For a watch meant to be worn daily for years, sapphire is generally worth the extra cost if it's within your budget.

Brand reputation is not the same as build quality

A recognizable name can carry real weight in resale value and design pedigree, but it doesn't automatically mean superior construction compared to a lesser-known maker using similar or better components. Looking at the actual specifications — movement type, crystal, water resistance, case material — tells you more about day-to-day quality than the name on the dial alone.

Consider the strap as a separate decision

Most watches can have their strap or bracelet swapped, so don't let a single strap option make or break a decision on an otherwise good watch. Leather offers a dressier look but needs more care around water exposure; steel bracelets are durable and versatile; rubber or fabric straps suit more casual or active use. Buying a watch with an easily interchangeable strap system gives you flexibility to adjust later.

Think about servicing before you buy

Mechanical watches need occasional professional servicing, typically every several years, to keep the movement running accurately and to replace worn lubricants. Before buying, it's worth checking whether the brand or a local watchmaker can service the specific movement, since some obscure or proprietary movements are harder and more expensive to service long-term than more common ones.

The takeaway

A good first quality watch comes down to matching movement type, case size, and water resistance to how you'll actually wear and use it, rather than chasing a specific brand name or trend. Try watches on in person when you can, and prioritize specifications that affect long-term durability and comfort over ones that are purely decorative.
