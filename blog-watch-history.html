HISTORY

A Short History of Wearing Time on Your Wrist

Wearing a clock on your wrist seems obvious today, but for a long stretch of history, the pocket watch was the dominant way people carried personal timekeeping, and wristwatches were considered a novelty, or worse, impractical.

Pocket watches came first, by centuries

Portable mechanical timepieces existed as pocket watches long before wristwatches became common, dating back to a period when portable clocks were rare, expensive, and primarily owned by the wealthy. Pocket watches were considered practical and appropriately masculine for daily carry, while early wristwatches were largely seen as women's jewelry rather than serious instruments.

Military necessity changed everything

The shift toward wristwatches accelerated significantly around the early twentieth century, driven largely by military necessity. Soldiers needed to check the time quickly, often while using both hands for other tasks, which made fumbling for a pocket watch impractical in combat conditions. Officers began strapping pocket watches to their wrists using leather cup fittings, and eventually watchmakers began producing watches designed specifically for wrist wear.

World War I cemented the shift

By the time of the First World War, wristwatches had moved from a battlefield improvisation to a standard-issue and widely adopted format. Soldiers returning from service continued wearing wristwatches in civilian life, and the format's practicality — quick to check, both hands free — meant it kept its foothold rather than reverting back to pocket watches once the war ended.

The quartz shift, decades later

For most of the twentieth century, mechanical movements were the only real option, and watchmaking centered heavily around mechanical craftsmanship and precision. In the 1970s, the introduction of quartz movements disrupted the industry significantly, offering far greater accuracy at a much lower cost, which briefly threatened the mechanical watch industry, especially in regions that had built their manufacturing around traditional movements.

Mechanical watches found a new identity

Rather than disappearing, mechanical watchmaking repositioned itself around craftsmanship, heritage, and the visible mechanical artistry, rather than competing on accuracy alone, where quartz had a clear technical advantage. This is part of why mechanical watches today are marketed and appreciated differently than quartz watches — less about pure function, more about the object and the craft behind it.

Smartwatches are a newer chapter, not a replacement

The rise of smartwatches in the past couple of decades introduced yet another shift, adding computing functions to the wrist rather than just timekeeping. Rather than eliminating traditional watches, smartwatches have largely carved out a separate category, with many people owning both a smartwatch for daily notifications and fitness tracking and a traditional mechanical or quartz watch for other occasions.

Why the wrist won over the pocket, long term

Beyond the military origin, the wristwatch's lasting popularity comes down to simple practicality: it's immediately visible without needing a free hand, it's harder to lose than a pocket watch that can slip out unnoticed, and it evolved into a visible style accessory in a way a pocket watch tucked away in fabric never could be. That combination of function and visibility is much of why the format has held on for over a century.

The takeaway

The wristwatch's dominance today feels inevitable in hindsight, but it took a specific set of historical pressures — mostly military necessity during wartime — to shift it from a niche, gendered accessory into the default way people carry personal time. Understanding that history adds a little more weight to what might otherwise feel like a purely functional object on your wrist.
