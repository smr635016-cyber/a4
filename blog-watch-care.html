Caring for a Mechanical Watch

A well-made mechanical watch can genuinely last a lifetime, but only with a handful of consistent care habits. None of them are complicated, but they're easy to overlook until a watch starts running poorly or stops entirely.

Winding a manual watch correctly

Manual watches need to be wound regularly, ideally at the same time each day, to keep the mainspring consistently tensioned. Wind gently until you feel resistance increase noticeably, and stop there rather than forcing it further, which can damage the mainspring or winding mechanism over time. Winding while the watch is off your wrist reduces stress on the crown and stem.

Keeping an automatic watch running

Automatic watches wind themselves through wrist motion, but they'll stop if left unworn for a day or two, depending on their power reserve. If you rotate between several automatic watches, a watch winder can keep one running while it's not being worn, though this isn't strictly necessary — simply resetting the time and date when you pick it back up is a perfectly reasonable alternative for most owners.

Setting the date safely

Many watches with a date function shouldn't have their date adjusted during a specific window, often between around 9 PM and 3 AM, because the date-change mechanism is mid-cycle during that period and forcing it can cause damage. If you're unsure of your specific watch's restricted window, moving the hands forward past midnight rather than adjusting the date directly during that stretch is a safer approach.

Water exposure and resistance ratings over time

A watch's water resistance rating reflects its condition when new or recently serviced, not a permanent guarantee. Gaskets and seals degrade over years, so a watch rated for swimming when purchased may need a fresh seal check after several years to maintain that same protection. If your watch sees regular water exposure, having the water resistance tested periodically by a watchmaker is a reasonable precaution.

Cleaning without damaging the case or crystal

A soft, slightly damp cloth is usually sufficient for cleaning the case and crystal of most watches, avoiding harsh chemicals or abrasive materials that can dull a finish or scratch a crystal. For a bracelet or metal strap, a soft brush can help clear dust or debris from between links, done carefully and without excessive water exposure unless the watch is rated for it.

Storage matters more than people expect

Storing a watch in direct sunlight for extended periods can fade a dial or degrade a leather strap over time. A dry, stable environment, away from strong magnetic fields like speakers or certain electronics, helps protect both the movement's accuracy and the materials. Magnetism in particular can affect a mechanical movement's timekeeping, sometimes requiring demagnetization by a watchmaker to correct.

Recognizing when a watch needs servicing

A mechanical watch that's consistently gaining or losing more time than usual, that feels different when winding, or that shows condensation under the crystal is signaling it needs professional attention. Most mechanical movements benefit from a full service, including cleaning and relubrication, every several years, even if nothing seems obviously wrong — waiting until a problem is visible often means more extensive and costly repair than a routine service would have required.

Leather strap care deserves its own attention

Leather straps are more vulnerable to water, sweat, and general wear than metal bracelets. Rotating between two straps if you wear a watch daily lets each one dry out and recover between wears, extending their life meaningfully compared to constant single-strap use. Avoid submerging a leather strap in water even if the watch case itself is water resistant.

The takeaway

Caring for a mechanical watch is mostly about consistency: wind or wear it regularly, avoid adjusting the date during the restricted window, keep it away from magnets and direct sun, and get it serviced on a reasonable schedule rather than only when something visibly goes wrong. These habits, more than any single expensive fix, are what let a good watch actually last for decades.
